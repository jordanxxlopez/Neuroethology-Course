Course Description

Neuroethology sits at the intersection of neuroscience, ethology, and evolutionary biology. It asks how nervous systems generate the behaviors that allow animals to find food, avoid predators, navigate across landscapes, and communicate with conspecifics, and how those neural solutions have been shaped by natural selection. NEUR 411 examines this question through a series of case studies, each centered on a single animal system: insect vision and hearing, the escape circuits of crayfish and fish, bat and owl sound localization, electroreception in sharks and weakly electric fish, magnetic and chemical navigation, songbird vocal learning, and primate communication. Within each case, we consider the behavior the animal must perform, the neural circuitry that performs it, and the experiments that established the mechanism.

The course is organized around Tinbergen's four questions (causation, development, function, and evolution), which provide a framework for distinguishing how a behavior works from how it develops, why it persists, and how it arose. Students will learn to evaluate the experimental evidence behind these claims, to recognize the limits of that evidence, and to compare how different taxa solve the same ecological problem. The course draws on more than 1,000 articles reviewed in designing the curriculum.

As the field evolves rapidly, this course is structured to cover as much current research as possible within a demanding schedule. Students should expect a substantial and sustained workload. By the end of the semester, students should be able to articulate, with supporting evidence, the neural capabilities that allow animals to detect prey, localize sounds, navigate long distances, and communicate with one another, and to assess how well current models explain those capabilities.

Required Field Experience: Spring Recess

The Spring Recess Field Experience is a required, four-day neuroethology field trip from Thursday, March 18 through Sunday, March 21, 2027. The trip is educational. You will evaluate animal welfare and behavior scientifically, using the methods and concepts from lecture. It is not recreational tourism. Your job is to observe, critique, and understand what animals’ environments and behaviors show about sensory processing, learning, stress, and enrichment. The host sites know we are coming and are prepared for us. There is no cost to students. All expenses are paid by the program. The trip is mandatory, and attendance on all four days is required.

  • Thursday, March 18: Beckman Center for Conservation Research & San Diego Zoo Safari Park (Escondido, CA).
  • Special topic: Animal Behavior in Managed Environments I: Zoos (sensory ecology, neural mechanisms of stress, social behavior, enrichment, and neuroethical considerations of captivity).
  • Friday, March 19: Hubbs-SeaWorld Research Institute & SeaWorld Rescue & Rehabilitation Center (San Diego, CA). We will carry out a neuroethical critique and behavioral audit.
  • Special topic: Animal Behavior in Managed Environments II: Aquariums & Marine Theme Parks (underwater sensory environments, learning and reward systems, social communication, and neuroethical considerations of training and performance).
  • Saturday, March 20: Aquarium of the Pacific (Long Beach).
  • Special topic: Animal Behavior in Managed Environments III: Aquarium Sensory Worlds (visual, chemical, and mechanosensory processing, sensory adaptations, and how exhibit design shapes natural behavior)
  • Sunday, March 21: Marine Mammal Care Center for a few hours. Then head back to USC around 1-2pm
  • Special topic: Animal Behavior in Managed Environments IV: Rescue, Rehabilitation & Release (neurobiology of stress, behavioral/injury recovery, human habituation, and assessment of readiness for release)

Overnight stays. We will stay overnight at the trip locations on each night of the trip, including the Aquarium of the Pacific. Bring a sleeping bag and whatever you need to sleep well, such as a sleeping pad, memory foam topper, pillow, and warm layers. The department will provide a limited number of sleeping bags from prior trips, but bring your own if you can.

Meals and schedule. Meals are provided by the program: breakfast at 9:00 AM, lunch at 1:00 PM, and dinner at 6:00 PM. For most of the day, you will have time to explore the sites and to learn directly from field biologists. After dinner, you will have free time.

Exams and evaluation. Field trip material will not be covered on exams. It will be evaluated through the Captivity and Conservation Reflection.

Absences. The trip is mandatory. An absence is excused only with documentation approved in advance. If you cannot attend or anticipate a conflict, contact me as soon as possible. The trip is costly and is planned in advance, so do not wait.

These four days appear in the Course Calendar as special topic sessions. We will meet as a class during the trip for informal discussions, animal observations, and group activities, with time to explore and enjoy the visits. Participation is required on all four days (attendance will be taken and incorporated into your lab participation grade). No assignments are due during the trip

Course Objectives

  • Explain how sensory systems extract ecologically relevant information from the environment, and how that information is converted into motor output.
  • Describe neural circuit mechanisms (identified neurons, central pattern generators, sensory maps, and neuromodulation) that generate specific animal behaviors.
  • Evaluate experimental evidence from behavioral, electrophysiological, anatomical, and lesion studies, including its limitations.
  • Compare solutions that different taxa use for the same problem, including convergent evolution.
  • Design and communicate a quantitative analysis or model of a natural behavior using lab data or published results.

Dr. J’s Grading Philosophy and Curve

Neuroethology requires you to connect neural mechanisms with animal behavior, interpret experimental evidence, and compare findings across species. This is a demanding upper-division course. Memorizing terminology alone is not sufficient; you must be able to explain how a circuit works, evaluate the evidence supporting a proposed mechanism, and apply your understanding to unfamiliar experimental results.

Exams and laboratory assignments reflect these expectations. Some assessments will be difficult, and raw percentages may be lower than you are accustomed to. Grades reflect demonstrated understanding, scientific accuracy, and the quality of your reasoning. Consistent effort and participation are important, but they do not substitute for meeting the course’s academic standards.

If the final class average, calculated using the weighted lecture and laboratory grades before curving, is below 70%, a curve will be applied at the end of the course. If the class average is 70% or higher, no curve will be applied. The size of any adjustment will depend on overall class performance. A curve accounts for assessment difficulty while maintaining expectations for mastery; it does not guarantee a particular letter grade or a passing grade. Use early assessment results to identify gaps in your understanding, review feedback, and seek help promptly. Do not rely on a possible curve to compensate for missing work or consistently weak performance. You must still earn at least 60% in both the lecture and laboratory components to pass the course.

Exams

There are four exams, each worth 120 points, and each covers exactly ten lectures. Exam 1 covers Lectures 1 to 10, Exam 2 covers Lectures 11 to 20, Exam 3 covers Lectures 21 to 30, and Exam 4 covers Lectures 31 to 40. Exams are not cumulative, but the concepts build on one another, so later exams will assume you still know earlier material. Exams are given on Mondays at 12:00 PM in NEU 100A or NEU 100B, and they start on time. Exam 4 is given during the final examination period. On the days of Exams 1, 2, and 3, the lecture is a special topic; special topics are not covered on any exam.

  • Number of questions and versions: Expect 80 questions on each exam. Each exam comes in three versions (V1, V2, and V3). The three versions contain the same questions, with the order of questions and answer choices mixed within each lecture section.
  • Question order: Exam questions are ordered by lecture. Questions on Lecture 1 come first, followed by questions on Lecture 2, and so on through the last lecture covered by that exam.
  • Room and version assignment: Before each exam, a randomized algorithm assigns every student to Room A (NEU 100A) or Room B (NEU 100B) and to one of three versions (V1, V2, or V3). Assignments are posted on the course site before the exam. Assignments are random, and requests to change them will not be accepted.
  • Exam format: In Person, Paper Exams consisting primarily of written free-response questions and fill-in-the-blank questions, with a small number of multiple-choice questions. Written questions may require short answers, longer explanations, interpretation of figures and data, experimental design critiques, and cross-species comparisons.
  • Expectations for answers: You must draw directly from lecture content, explain specific experimental findings, and provide relevant examples of animals, behaviors, and neural mechanisms. When asked to support an explanation, identify the evidence and explain how it supports your answer. Memorizing terminology or repeating slide text without demonstrating understanding is not sufficient.
  • Missed exams: Make-up exams are not given. If you miss one exam, a zero will initially be recorded. If the absence is approved for a valid, documented medical or official reason, that zero will be replaced at the end of the semester by the average of your other three exam scores. This replacement uses your other exam scores, not your final course grade. An undocumented or unapproved absence will remain a zero.
  • Bring a photo ID and a pencil (YOU WILL BE VERIFIED). Phones, smartwatches, and notes are not allowed
  • Exams are closed-note unless the class negotiates an alternative at the beginning of the semester (and/or before Exam I). The class may negotiate permission to bring one 8.5 × 11-inch handwritten reference sheet (double sided, OK). In exchange, the class agrees to replace the multiple-choice questions with additional fill in the blank questions (any change needs to be negotiated with the class as a whole). Reference sheets must be handwritten; typed or printed material is not permitted.

New lecture content is introduced on exam days. These lectures are shorter, but attendance is required, and the material will be covered on the next exam.

Lecture Project (20% of Lecture Grade)

Each student completes one project based on an animal system covered in class. Choose ONE option. Your choice is due with your proposal.

Option A: Simulation Project

Build a computational simulation of a neural circuit, sensory system, or behavior from one animal covered in lecture (for example, the cricket phonotaxis circuit, the Mauthner startle circuit, a CPG, or the owl ITD map). Models must be implemented in Python, MATLAB, or an equivalent tool. Deliverables: the working code (commented and runnable), a 5-page written report with the model's assumptions, parameter justification, validation against published data, and a discussion of where it fails, and a 5-minute recorded walkthrough. A model that cannot be compared with published data will not earn more than 70 percent of the project points.

Option B: 10-Page APA Paper

Write a 10-page paper in APA 7th edition format, excluding title page, abstract, and references, on one animal covered in class. The paper must synthesize at least 8 primary research articles, explain the neural mechanism and the behavior it produces, and critically evaluate the evidence. Summaries of individual papers will receive low marks. The paper must argue something.

Laboratory Component

The lab is a required, graded component and is not optional. Labs use recorded and published data, preserved specimens, and, where approved, live animal observation carried out under an institutional animal care protocol. You will complete animal welfare and lab safety training before Lab 1. Mishandling of animals, data, or equipment results in removal from the lab and a failing lab grade.

  • Lab Reports (10 labs, lowest 2 dropped, 7.5% each for the 8 counted). Each report follows a standard format: Introduction, Methods, Results (with figures and statistics), Discussion, and References. Reports must be to 6-10 pages. Every quantitative claim requires a statistical test and an interpretation.
  • Lab Attendance and Participation (10%). Attendance is required at every lab. Participation is earned by submitting your observations and data to Gradescope before you leave the lab. Submissions made after you leave will not be marked, and you will receive no participation credit for that lab. Excused absences require documentation submitted to the TA within 24 hours. Each unexcused absence reduces this grade by 15 points out of 100.
  • Computational Assignments (3 assignments, 5% each, 15% total). Short Python or MATLAB problems covering data analysis and simulation that build the coding skills required for the simulation project. Due dates are in the schedule below.
  • Captivity and Conservation Reflection (15%). A 5-page essay on your experience during the Spring Recess Field Experience and what you learned. Describe what you observed, evaluate the welfare and behavioral evidence scientifically, and explain what the visit taught you about how captive environments shape behavior. The essay is due Sat Apr 24. It is the best opportunity in the course to show you understand the biology, and it is also the one most students skip. Do not skip it.
  • Research use of this essay: This essay is part of research on young students participating in active research, studying learning accessibility and disparities. Use of your essay in that research is voluntary. Declining has no effect on your grade or on how your essay is scored. This statement applies only to the Captivity and Conservation Reflection.

Lecture Recordings and Study Materials

Lectures are recorded. Recordings are released one week before the exam that covers them. I may release them two weeks before an exam at my discretion, but early release is not guaranteed and should not be expected. You are responsible for attending lecture, taking thorough notes, and studying throughout the semester. Do not wait for recordings to begin preparing for an exam.

Recordings from previous offerings may also be posted as supplemental study materials. This course changes between semesters, and older recordings may omit current topics or include material that is no longer covered. This semester’s lectures determine what is examinable. You are responsible for identifying those differences using the current lectures and slides.

Recordings do not replace attendance or excuse arriving unprepared. Lecture content is dense and requires consistent engagement. Choosing to rely on recordings does not reduce the material you are expected to know or change the standards by which your work will be graded.

Study Expectations

This course covers a lot of material on a fast schedule, and it will punish procrastination. Plan at least two hours of outside study for every hour of class. Review each lecture within 48 hours. Read the recommended papers for each lecture in the Readings Calendar. Work through the figures until you can explain the data without looking at the caption. Form study groups but make sure each of you can answer exam questions alone. Do not wait until the week of an exam to begin studying, and do not start the project the week it is due.

Lecture & Lab Attendance

Attendance at all lectures and labs is expected. Lectures introduce material that is not on the slides, and that material will appear on exams. There is no grade for lecture attendance, but missing lectures will affect your exam performance. Lab attendance is graded, and lab absences will not be excused without documentation.

Late Work

  • Lab reports, computational assignments, and the Captivity and Conservation Reflection: 5% deduction per day late for the first 7 days. Work more than 1 week late is not accepted.
  • Lab participation: observations and data must be submitted to Gradescope before you leave the lab. Submissions after you leave are not marked.
  • Lecture project: 30% deduction for any submission after 11:59 PM on the due date. Projects submitted more than 48 hours late are not accepted.

Extensions are granted only for documented medical or emergency circumstances, and only if you contact me before the deadline. Asking for an extension the night before a deadline is not an emergency.

Academic Integrity and Use of AI Tools

Follow the university's standards of academic integrity. Cheating, plagiarism, unauthorized collaboration, and the use of unauthorized materials on exams will result in a zero on the work and a referral under university policy. Generative AI tools may not write any part of the lab reports, Captivity and Conservation Reflection, lecture project paper, or exam answers. You may use AI tools for coding assistance on the simulation project only if you disclose the use in your methods section and reference. Undisclosed use is a violation.

Communication

Because this course involves complex, interdisciplinary concepts related to neural mechanisms and animal behavior, questions and clarification needs are expected. Students are strongly encouraged to use Teaching Assistants as their first point of contact for questions related to homework, labs, coding issues, and course logistics. TAs hold regular lab sessions and office hours and are well positioned to provide timely support. For questions that cannot be resolved with a TA, or for concerns related to unresolved grading disputes, accommodations, or broader course issues, students may contact the instructor at jlopez11@usc.edu.

I will make every effort to respond to emails and messages promptly; however, because I teach multiple courses and run an active research laboratory, response times may be delayed. Students should generally expect responses within one business day. Messages sent in the evening, overnight, or on weekends will typically be addressed the following business day. Use clear subject lines that include the course number and topic, such as “NEUR 411: Lab 3 report question.” Keep questions concise and include relevant context, such as the assignment name or lab section. Reach out early rather than waiting until deadlines or exam days. Consult the syllabus and current lecture materials for exam coverage before emailing.

Absences and Communication

I understand that many students balance academic responsibilities with work, family obligations, and other commitments. Communication is key. If you anticipate an absence or experience a documented emergency, please email me as soon as possible. For lab absences, also notify your TA and submit documentation within 24 hours, as required by the laboratory attendance policy. Being proactive and communicative helps me support you throughout the semester.

Unexcused lab absences will affect your Lab Attendance and Participation grade under the laboratory attendance policy, regardless of access to lecture recordings. There is no separate lecture attendance grade, but you remain responsible for all missed lecture content. Recordings are provided as a supplement and do not replace in-person participation. Missed exams remain subject to the missed-exam policy.

Final Word

This course is hard, and it is designed to be. Neuroethology asks you to think like a biologist, an engineer, and a skeptic at the same time. If you do the reading, show up to lecture and lab, and start early, you will leave with a working understanding of how animals sense and act on their worlds. If you do not, the schedule will catch up with you by the first exam. Plan accordingly.
